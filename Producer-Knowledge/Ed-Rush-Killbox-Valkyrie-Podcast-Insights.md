# Ed Rush & Killbox — Valkyrie Podcast 001

> Long-form interview hosted by Tom (Tangents) from Valkyrie with Ed Rush (EdRush/Optical, one of the founding figures of neurofunk) and Gareth (Killbox), released alongside their comeback EP "Killer of Killers" on Valkyrie's label Killer of Killers. Wide-ranging conversation covering scene history, the pirate-radio/free-party era, studio and label economics, collaboration workflow, sound-design philosophy, and AI in music production. Filtered here for the durable career/production/life lessons — this is one of the richer "lived experience" sources in the vault, worth resurfacing whenever motivation, workflow, or business-mindset questions come up.

## Career & Mindset

- **The grind is the point, not an obstacle.** Both describe years of unglamorous, unpaid effort before any payoff — Ed Rush worked multiple manual-labor jobs (wine delivery, butcher, fishmonger) purely to fund decks and studio time; Gareth spent ~4 months locked in his room just learning to beatmatch on drifting turntables while friends went out. The obsession itself was treated as non-negotiable — "failure wasn't an option."
- **Sacrifice is part of the skill, not a side effect.** Time, social life, and sometimes relationships get sacrificed in the early years. That sacrifice is framed as *teaching* discipline, not just costing you something — the people who weren't willing to make that trade filtered themselves out naturally (couldn't afford the gear, wouldn't skip the party).
- **No shortcuts era vs. now.** Pre-internet, there was no tutorial for anything — just reverse-engineering other producers' records by ear, figuring out *why* something worked. Both are careful not to romanticize this uncritically: today's accessibility (YouTube, Patreon, tutorials) is genuinely good, but the exploration and struggle of figuring things out yourself is where the real learning happens — jumping straight from question to answer skips the part that builds a producer's instincts.
- **The long journey builds skills the fast path doesn't.** Someone who gets a hit early and a suddenly full diary may lack the accumulated social/professional skills (how to talk to promoters, handle a crowded green room, manage a live crisis) that come from years of grinding through smaller gigs.
- **Character shows in small moments.** How an artist treats a waiter or door staff reveals more than their studio output — repeatedly cited as a genuine predictor of who has staying power in the scene versus who flames out.
- **Re-learning is harder than learning.** Ed Rush's transition from analog/hardware-era engineering into fully digital production (accelerated by COVID lockdown time) required actively *unlearning* old hardware-era habits that didn't map onto DAW workflows, not just adding new skills on top.
- **Purpose is existential, not just professional.** Closing thought on AI: humans need purpose/value to "stay sane and get out of bed" — used as the frame for why creative work matters beyond output.

## Production Philosophy

- **Constraint breeds creativity — "less is more."** Gareth deliberately keeps his effects rack small: one main reverb, one or two delays, a couple of distortion units. Argument: an overstocked plugin folder invites tweaking-as-procrastination and outsourcing your sound to a plugin's "signature," rather than the artist actually working the idea. Comparison used: an artist doesn't obsess over which paintbrush just came out; a good mechanic works with a limited, familiar toolkit.
- **The journey from A to B is the creative part.** Distortion/sound-design chains are valued for the *process* of pushing a sound through multiple stages (EQ → distort → EQ → modulate) rather than reaching for one plugin that does the whole job in one step — jumping straight to the end result skips the discoveries made along the way.
- **Tape-era commitment vs. infinite-recall culture.** Analog mixing forced permanent decisions — no undo, no recall, sometimes not even a phone to photograph the desk settings (grease-pencil marks on faders instead). The constraint is described positively: catching "the magic in the bottle," a session where the track seems to mix itself because you're fully locked in (flow state), rather than endlessly re-litigating a mix.
- **New tools are worth adopting only when they change what's possible, not just what's convenient.** Named example: a sidechain plugin that introduced new (e.g. ring-modulation-style) sidechain behavior was called essential once discovered — the bar for adding a new permanent tool to the template is "this does something I genuinely couldn't do before," not just "this is another way to do the same thing."
- **Trusted collaborators change the process.** Killbox's writing process shifted over the years from always being in the same room together to a stem-bouncing back-and-forth workflow. Benefits of remote/async: each person can nerd out on a section for hours without feeling like they're wasting a collaborator's time, and anticipation of the other person's reaction becomes its own creative fuel. Benefits of in-room collaboration: real-time energy and instant feedback, especially with more than two people in a room. Trust in a collaborator's taste is what makes async work — you stop worrying whether what comes back will be good.
- **Consistent monitoring/reference habits enable remote collaboration.** Because both had converged on similar studio calibration/reference habits over years, stems sent between their two rooms translated reliably — a shared technical foundation is what actually makes handoff-based collaboration fast.
- **Every genre you touch outside your main lane feeds back into it.** Both describe writing house, hip-hop/trap, and other non-DnB material as expanding their sound palette — not as a distraction from the "main" genre. Working with vocal melody and chord emotion (during a trap/R&B side project) forced a different creative approach than "will this work on a dancefloor," which fed back into more melodic/emotive album tracks later.
- **Sound sources: both planned and found.** Sample-hunting sometimes starts with a track title/concept and a deliberate search; other times it's stumbling on an old sample while browsing a hard drive and hearing it differently than before. A single sample can be either the seed that starts a whole track's direction, or the final "cherry on top" piece that completes an idea already mostly written.
- **A strong concept (e.g. an EP or track title) constrains and directs the whole writing process** — deciding on "Killer of Killers" as the EP name reportedly ruled out entire subgenres (no liquid, no minimal rollers) before a single sound was chosen, keeping the palette coherent.

## Business & Scene Structure

- **Running a label "properly" is a huge, ongoing commitment** — needs a team, dedicated time, and usually loses money early on. Several artists (Ed Rush's Piranha Pool, Gareth's Snake Pit) describe starting a label almost reluctantly, partly from external/self-imposed pressure ("shouldn't I have a label by now?") rather than a fully formed plan — and consciously choosing to keep it small/occasional rather than treat it as a serious commercial operation while still touring and producing.
- **Stepping back from your own production to run a label has a real cost** in a fast-moving subgenre — step off the train for even a year and the sound has moved on enough that catching back up takes real effort. This is given as a deliberate reason for prioritizing staying an active producer over building out a label full-time.
- **Crews/collectives (like Valkyrie) exist to let specialists focus on what they're best at** rather than every artist trying to also be a full-time label manager, distributor, and social media team.
- **Physical/collectible culture (vinyl, dub-plate culture) is explicitly framed as a community-retention mechanism**, not nostalgia for its own sake — it gives fans something tangible to invest in and gives artists another creative surface (art, one-off variants) beyond the audio itself.
- **Recognition matters more than money for a lot of long-standing artists** — several times in the conversation, a single specific fan comment ("nice to hear someone play new music I hadn't heard") is described as more meaningful than commercial metrics.

## Scene Culture & Community

- **The rave/community feeling is described as a deliberate design goal, not a side effect** — physical connection, recognizing people from an online group in person, and low-friction approachability (being "just a guy," not gatekept) are treated as things a label/crew can and should actively cultivate, not things that just happen.
- **Social media is framed as simultaneously connecting and isolating** — more reach, but less of the unplanned physical proximity and shared-risk culture (free parties, pirate radio) that originally built the scene's community feel.
- **Historical throughline**: free-party/pirate-radio culture → sound-clash MC/hosting traditions → dub-plate culture of unreleased exclusives → today's private community chat groups. Framed as the same underlying impulse (exclusivity + shared secret + belonging) expressed through whatever the era's technology allows.

## On AI in Music (their stated position at time of recording)

- Both draw a distinction between **using AI as a tool inside a human-directed process** (e.g., text-to-speech to clean up/repurpose a vocal sample they already chose and are shaping, or splitting stems from an existing film clip) versus **using AI to generate the creative direction itself** (prompting for "a hard neuro track in the style of X with a breakdown like Y") — the former is acceptable to them, the latter is explicitly rejected as removing the human authorship that gives the work meaning.
- Underlying fear articulated: if AI ever convincingly encodes human emotional experience (grief, heartbreak) into generateable output, that would remove the thing that makes human-made music worth making — lived experience as the non-reproducible ingredient.
- Stated position: AI is welcome for automating "mundane" tasks that free humans up for creative work, but is a problem the moment it starts replacing the *creative* decision-making itself.
- Concern also raised about a broader "outsourced imagination" effect — worry that leaning on AI/prompting early and often could blunt the kind of internally-generated imagination that unstructured childhood play and analog media consumption used to build.
